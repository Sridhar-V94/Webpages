6 · Critical considerations & methodology

Things people usually overlook
  • You must actually invest the difference. The entire renting case rests on investing every rupee of (EMI − rent) every month. Set “investment discipline” below 100% to see how quickly the advantage disappears.
  • Transaction friction is brutal. Stamp duty + registration + brokerage at entry and exit can be ~7–10% of the property value. The flat must appreciate meaningfully just to get back to break-even.
  • Leverage cuts both ways. A ₹1.5 crore loan on a ₹2 crore home is 75% LTV. A 5% price fall wipes out roughly 20% of your equity, while the loan balance does not move.
  • Rental yields in India are low. ₹50,000/month on a ₹2 crore flat is a 3% gross yield. You are paying for the right to live there plus an appreciation bet.
  • Maintenance is not just the EMI. Society charges, repairs, property tax and periodic renovations commonly run 1–2% of property value per year and rise with inflation.
  • Tax rules change. Section 80C / 24 interest deduction exist only in the old regime. Equity LTCG, indexation and standard deduction rules have changed repeatedly — model a range, not one point.
  • Liquidity and concentration. A buyer is asset-rich but cash-poor; a renter holds a liquid, diversified portfolio. Selling a flat can take months in a weak market.
  • Flexibility has value. Renting lets you move for a job, school or a better neighbourhood without a huge exit cost. That option value is real even though it is not in the spreadsheet.
  • After the loan ends, the comparison changes. The buyer stops paying EMI but still pays upkeep. If you keep investing the old EMI amount after loan closure, the renter's corpus must be compared fairly — use the toggle provided.
  • Inflation and sequence risk. EMI is fixed in rupees; rent and upkeep rise. Investment returns are not smooth — a poor decade just before you need the money can change the answer.
  • Security and emotion matter. Rent escalation, eviction risk and the desire for a stable home are not financial line items but can outweigh a few lakh of difference.
How the model works

EMI = P × r × (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1), with r = annual rate / 1200 and n = tenure in months. Each month interest = balance × r and principal = EMI − interest, so the loan balance amortises correctly.

Buyer net worth = property value × (1 − selling cost %) − home loan outstanding + corpus built from reinvested tax savings − one-time stamp duty, registration & other upfront costs.

Renter net worth = investment corpus. It starts with the down payment (and optionally the same upfront transaction amount) and each month receives (EMI − current rent) × investment discipline. Rent grows at the rent-inflation rate. If rent exceeds the investable amount, the shortfall is withdrawn from the corpus.

Real values divide nominal net worth by (1 + general inflation)^years. Exit tax is applied only to gains above contributions (an approximation of capital gains tax; exemptions are ignored).

Break-even year is the first year where buyer net worth ≥ renter net worth. Break-even appreciation and break-even return are found by binary search on the selected horizon.

Indian tax assumptions used

Section 80C principal deduction assumed capped at ₹1,50,000/year and Section 24 interest deduction at ₹2,00,000/year, valued at your marginal tax rate. These apply only under the old tax regime; turn the toggle off for the new regime. Property capital gains on sale and LTCG on investments are only approximated by the single exit-tax slider — real treatment depends on holding period, indexation and exemptions. Consult a tax advisor for decisions.

Interpretation caveats

This is a deterministic planning model, not a forecast. It assumes constant returns and growth rates, ignores GST, rental deposits, clubhouse/renovation levies, insurance and loan processing fees unless you add them via “other upfront costs”. It also assumes you can invest the monthly difference with the same discipline as your EMI. Results are indicative only and not financial advice.
